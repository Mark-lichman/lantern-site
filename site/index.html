A bedtime book for kids under 12

It starts with what she loves, sends her off the screen, and lets her keep the story going.

Each night Lantern writes one short page, opens a real book, and gives her one small thing to make with her hands. Then it closes itself.

For grown-ups only. We never collect anything from children.

One night with Lantern

About fifteen minutes on the screen, then the real work happens off it. Here is Maya's first night. She's nine, she loves monsters, and she has been told she reads below her grade.

  1. 01

    The book opens

    It asks her name, then one thing she already likes. It never asks what she can read, and it never will.

  2. 02

    Something writes in the margin

    A grey pencil voice that says she can't. She rubs it out with a finger and gives it a name. Naming a thing is how you stop being afraid of it.

  3. 03

    Three doors

    Three real books: one from home ground, one a step out, one two steps out. The book decides what's offered. She decides where it goes.

  4. 04

    The way in

    Eighty words of setup and a hook, so she opens the book with a question she needs answered. Then one quest. Never a list.

  5. 05

    The lantern goes out

    "I'll be here when you get back. Don't hurry." Then the app closes itself. No countdown, no notification, nothing to break.

  6. 06

    She reads, then she makes something

    A whole real book, then one page of her own field guide: find a thing nobody looks at, draw it, name it. Paper and a pencil, nothing else.

Night four

When she doesn't do it.

Some nights the quest doesn't happen. Lantern doesn't count it, doesn't remind her, and never shows her a list of what she owes. A backlog is a guilt machine for a kid.

On a hard week the doors quietly move back toward what she already loves. She's never told. Nothing looks different. The book just stops pushing for a while.

A comfort zone that only ever widens isn't comfort. It's a ratchet.

Every night ends. Her story keeps going.

What makes it stick isn't a number of nights. It's the feeling she gets the second she finishes something real, so Lantern celebrates right away and keeps every task small enough to do on her worst day.

It widens only as fast as her confidence grows. A door she picks twice becomes home ground. That's how a kid walks from monsters to a mountain king without one night that felt like a stretch.

There's no finish line. Each night closes itself, and the next one picks up where her story left off. Her field guide gets a little thicker every time, a real thing made by hand that she keeps working on for as long as she wants to.

What Lantern will never do

These aren't settings. They're how it's built, and anything that breaks one is a decision, not a patch.

  • Store, send or show anyone what a child says or picks
  • Give points, badges, streaks, leaderboards or reading levels
  • Send notifications, or give her any reason to open it more often
  • Quiz her, test her, or check whether she read the book
  • Show a grown-up what the voice in her margin said
  • Let children contact other children. No chat, no names, no free text.
